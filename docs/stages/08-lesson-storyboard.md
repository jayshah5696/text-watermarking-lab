# Stage 8 lesson storyboard

> Status: pre-run. Measured beats remain blank until Stage 8 evidence verifies.

## One question

How does ordinary editing change the evidence frozen in Stage 7, and what does a larger generation
bias buy?

## Continuity rule

Open on the exact Stage 7 rank `1000` marked copied string, its ordered token strip, and its 80-token
`G/T`, z, and strict cutoff. Do not redraw it as generic prose.

The character string is the first object. Token boundaries sit under it. Keyed membership attaches
to token ID plus previous token ID. When an edit changes either identity, stop matching the old and
new token objects. Never slide old green/red labels onto new positions.

Rank `1001` remains the inconvenient row. It was already equal to its control through 80 copied
tokens. Keep it near cohort results so the page never implies all rows start with strong evidence.

## Beat order

### 1. Reopen the frozen Stage 7 output

Show rank 1000, exact copied text, first 80 copied token IDs, unedited `G/T`, z, and cutoff state.
Label Stage 7 values as measured and unchanged.

Why now: the learner needs to see that Stage 8 edits recorded evidence rather than generating a
cleaner example.

### 2. State the editing problem in ordinary words

Place one blank edited-string lane under the source. Ask what the checker actually receives after a
word is removed. Answer with the literal edited characters, not a token count or abstract attack.

Why now: editing acts on text before it acts on the detector.

### 3. Delete one preselected word

Reveal one deletion from the fixed 10 percent condition. Cross out the exact word in coral, close
the text gap, and keep all untouched characters visible.

Interaction: Play, Pause, Previous, Next, Replay. Autoplay reveals deterministic deletions. Manual
interaction pauses autoplay. Reduced motion starts paused.

Why now: one visible change is easier to follow than an attack summary.

### 4. Re-tokenize both strings

Draw tokenizer boundaries below the old and edited strings. Match token objects only while position,
ID, and decoded piece remain identical. Mark the first mismatch. Split the lanes afterward.

Why now: the learner can now see why deletion affects more than one word-shaped box.

### 5. Replay the key on the edited history

For each edited eligible token, show previous ID, current ID, and keyed result. Build the edited
`G/T` count one token at a time. Keep the key chip fixed above both lanes.

Why now: the score change now has a visible causal chain.

### 6. Calculate one edited score

Use concrete Stage 8 values. Show ordinary hits, ordinary movement, edited hits, and z. Move the same
z marker from its Stage 7 position to its edited position. Keep the strict cutoff fixed.

Why now: notation follows the operation and count.

### 7. Add the success gate

Place three separate checks beside the score:

- detector evidence changed;
- enough copied length remains; and
- the declared meaning screen passes.

Do not collapse them into one badge. If the spine deletion fails preservation, say so beside its
lower score.

Why now: a score reduction can be bought by destroying the text.

### 8. Compare one named edit at a time

Prescribed order: normalization, homoglyph 1 percent, homoglyph 5 percent, deletion 10 percent,
deletion 30 percent, mixing 25 percent, mixing 50 percent, paraphrase.

For each tab, state what stays fixed, what changes, what to watch, and one plain result sentence.
Keep advanced row selection hidden until all attack types have been visited.

Why now: the learner has enough machinery to interpret an attack without treating the page as a
dashboard.

### 9. Pause on homoglyphs

Show source and replacement Unicode code points for the spine row. Put the visual-readability warning
beside the result. State that this diagnoses tokenizer sensitivity and does not demonstrate semantic
paraphrasing.

Why now: visually similar characters can hide a large tokenization change.

### 10. Pause on paraphrase

Show source, rewrite, achieved length ratio, number preservation, embedding cosine, and manual review
status. Explain each screen before showing the detector change. If any screen fails or is uncertain,
block the phrase "meaning-preserving removal."

Why now: a generative rewrite adds a second model behavior and needs its own evidence.

### 11. Reveal every attack row

For one attack, show all 12 paired z changes before adding a mean or median. Highlight rank 1000,
rank 1001, and the first score-reducing row that fails a preservation check. Keep row and attack
identities visible.

Why now: the aggregate must not erase starting overlap or damaged outputs.

### 12. Return to generation and change delta alone

Restore the exact Stage 7 prompt, seed, model, sampler, key, and cap for rank 1000. Show three calls.
Delta 2 is the frozen Stage 7 record. Delta 1 and 3 are the only new calls.

Interaction: `Compare delta calls` fades every identical field and leaves `bias=1`, `bias=2`, or
`bias=3`.

Why now: editing and stronger embedding are distinct interventions.

### 13. Follow one bias row through evidence and proxies

For rank 1000, reveal achieved copied length, 80-token z, conditional NLL, repetition, distinct-2,
and distinct-3 at delta 1, 2, and 3. Define each proxy in one sentence. Never combine them into one
quality score.

Why now: the learner can read what was purchased and what proxy moved.

### 14. Reveal all eight bias rows

Show raw row lines across delta before summary marks. Preserve short or missing 80-token points.
Aligned panels keep detector z, NLL, repetition, and achieved length separate.

Why now: one clean row cannot support a trade-off claim.

### 15. State the measured answer narrowly

Use artifact-backed language for this 12-row edit fixture and 8-row bias sweep. Put failed
preservation cases next to any removal count. End with the configured positive wording and the list
of untested claims.
