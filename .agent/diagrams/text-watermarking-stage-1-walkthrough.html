Text watermarking lab · Stage 1

Start with a weighted coin.

We want to learn one idea: a small, repeated preference can be hard to see in a short sample and easy to measure in a long one. We will build the checker one piece at a time, then map every piece back to generated text.

First, no formulasWe flip two coins and watch what changes.
Then, one exampleWe explain every number in an 80-flip result.

01 · The actual job

What are we trying to detect?

A text generator chooses one small piece of text at a time. During generation, a watermark can slightly favor a secret group of valid choices. Someone who knows the same secret can later count how often the finished text used those choices.

A detector is a small calculator.

It counts selected positions, compares the count with an ordinary average, and returns a score. A fixed cutoff turns that score into a possible-signal result.

It does not read the meaning of the passage. It does not decide who wrote it. It tests for one watermark pattern that was configured in advance.

1

Generate

A secret rule slightly favors some valid next-token choices.

2

Count

The checker uses the same rule on the finished text and counts favored choices.

3

Compare

It asks whether the extra count is larger than ordinary random variation.

The claim is narrow. A positive result means “consistent with this configured watermark and secret key.” It does not mean “AI-written.”
02 · Replace text with coin flips

Two sources. One small difference.

Stage 1 removes language from the problem. Each future token choice becomes one coin flip. Heads means the source made a favored choice. Tails means it made another valid choice.

These are weighted coins, not fair 50/50 coins. The baseline coin lands heads 25% of the time. The nudged coin lands heads 40% of the time. Here, p simply means “chance of heads on the next flip.” We use 25% because the later watermark experiment places about one quarter of the available token choices in its secret favored set.

04 · See ordinary variation

The same baseline coin gives different counts.

Below are 20 batches from the baseline coin. Each batch contains 80 flips. The bar height is the number of heads. Most counts sit near 20, but chance moves them around.

Standard deviation names the usual amount of movement. For 80 baseline flips, it is 3.87 heads. This does not set a hard boundary. It gives us a useful unit for distance from the average.
05 · Give distance a name

A z-score is distance in “usual variation” units.

Instead of saying “12 extra heads,” we say how large that excess is compared with normal baseline movement. That lets us compare results of different lengths on one scale.

20 headsz = 0
24 headsz ≈ 1
28 headsz ≈ 2
32 headsz = 3.10

Read the score as a sentence.

z = 3.10 means: “The observed head count is 3.10 standard deviations above the baseline average.”

z = 0 means the count equals the baseline average. It does not prove that the source is baseline. It only describes the count.

06 · Turn a score into a decision

The checker needs a cutoff.

A score alone does not say yes or no. Before looking at a result, we choose a line. In this lab, the checker reports a possible signal when z ≥ 3.

Our 80-flip example

score 3.10≥cutoff 3

The score crosses the line. The checker raises an alarm.

What this decision means

32 or more heads is rare under the baseline coin.

The exact baseline coin model produces 32 or more heads in 80 flips about 0.22% of the time. Rare does not mean impossible, so the result is evidence rather than proof.

One run gives a score. Many runs make two hills.

Each simulated batch produces one z-score. The blue hill shows scores from 2,000 baseline batches. The orange hill shows 2,000 nudged batches. The vertical red line is the cutoff. Every score to its right raises an alarm.

11 · The committed Stage 1 experiment

Longer nudged sequences are easier to catch.

The repository runs 10,000 baseline sequences and 10,000 nudged sequences at each length, using the fixed 25% baseline, 40% nudge, and z = 3 cutoff. These are results from the toy coin experiment, not claims about a real language model.

Read the chart

Orange: nudged sequences caught by the checker.

Blue: baseline sequences that raised a false alarm.

At 40 flips, most nudged sequences are missed. At 400 flips, the two sources are easy to separate under this simple model.

The persistent excess grows in direct proportion to length. Baseline variation grows more slowly, in proportion to the square root of length.

12 · Optional math and history

Where did the formula come from?

It was not invented for text watermarks. It is the standard score applied to a binomial count: the number of heads in a fixed number of independent yes-or-no trials.

See the exact formula, now that each part has a meaning

For T flips and baseline head chance γ, the binomial model gives an average of Tγ heads and a standard deviation of √(Tγ(1−γ)). We subtract the average from the observed count G, then divide by the standard deviation.

z = (G − Tγ) / √(Tγ(1 − γ))

T is the number of flips. G is the observed number of heads. γ is the baseline chance of heads. Statisticians often call the baseline model the null hypothesis.

The normal approximation to binomial counts was developed through work by Abraham de Moivre and later Pierre-Simon Laplace. The basic mean and standard-deviation formulas come from the binomial model itself. For the committed fixed-length checks, the lab also compares its result with the exact binomial upper tail.

13 · What we learned, and what we did not

This stage proves a mechanism, not a product.

Learned

A persistent probability shift can be detected by counting hits and comparing them with baseline variation.

Measured

With these locked toy settings, more flips sharply improve the chance of catching the nudged source.

Not yet tested

No tokenizer, language model, real text, editing attack, secret-key implementation, or production detector is involved in Stage 1.
